The Evolution of Optical Storage

From the first compact disc to the age of streaming — how humanity learned to store light, then quietly traded quality for convenience.

Explore the Timeline Compare the Formats
01 — Timeline

Four moments that changed how we store media

Each format solved a real problem of its era. Each one was eventually declared obsolete — usually before it actually was.

1982
The Compact Disc launches

Philips and Sony release the CD in Japan and Europe. A 120 mm polycarbonate disc holds 700 MB — or 74 minutes of uncompressed audio, a length reportedly chosen so Beethoven's Ninth Symphony would fit on one disc.

1995
DVD brings the cinema home

With 4.7 GB per layer (8.5 GB on dual-layer discs), the DVD delivers full-length films with far better picture than VHS, plus multiple audio tracks, subtitles and chapter menus.

2006
Blu-ray wins the high-definition war

A blue-violet 405 nm laser reads far smaller pits, pushing capacity to 25 GB per layer and 50 GB on dual-layer discs — enough for true 1080p and later 4K Ultra HD.

2007
Streaming arrives

Netflix launches its streaming service. Convenience wins the decade — but the bitrate never caught up with the disc, and the trade-off is still visible on any large screen today.

04 — Why Physical Still Wins

The advantages of CD and Blu-ray

Two formats, four decades apart, share one property that streaming structurally cannot offer: permanence.

Real ownership

A disc is yours. A streaming licence can be revoked, edited or removed without notice — and regularly is.

No internet required

No buffering, no bandwidth caps, no server outages. The disc works in 2036 exactly as it does today.

Far higher bitrate

Blu-ray delivers up to 128 Mbps with lossless audio; typical 4K streaming sits between 15 and 25 Mbps.

Universal compatibility

A CD pressed in 1985 still plays in a 2026 car stereo. Try that with a 1985 file format.

Built for the long term

Blu-ray's hard-coating resists scratches, and archival discs are rated for decades rather than subscription cycles.

05 — The Trade-Off

What digital content quietly costs you

Streaming is convenient. It is also, measurably, a downgrade — and most viewers have never seen the comparison side by side.

Not truly high definition

A 4K stream compresses to roughly a fifth of a 4K Blu-ray's data. Dark scenes, film grain and fine detail are the first casualties.

You rent, you don't own

Titles leave catalogues when licences expire. Your "library" is a list of permissions, not a collection.

Dependence on infrastructure

Outages, throttling, regional restrictions and data caps all sit between you and the content you already pay for.

Subscriptions stack up

Four or five monthly services cost more per year than building a shelf of discs you keep forever.

Platforms can disappear

When a service shuts down, purchases go with it. Physical media has no such single point of failure.
